# Could aging evolve as a pathogen control strategy?

**Peter V. Lidsky and Raul Andino**

*Department of Immunology and Microbiology, University of California, San Francisco, San Francisco, CA 94158, USA*

**Published in:** Trends in Ecology & Evolution, December 2022, Vol. 37, No. 12

---

## Abstract

Aging is often attributed to the detrimental side effects of beneficial traits but not a programmed adaptive process. Alternatively, the pathogen control hypothesis posits that defense against infectious diseases may provide a strong selection force for restriction of lifespan. Aging might have evolved to remove older individuals who carry chronic diseases that may transmit to their younger kin. Thus, selection for shorter lifespans may benefit kin's fitness. The pathogen control hypothesis addresses arguments typically raised against adaptive aging concepts: it explains the benefit of shorter lifespan and the absence of mutant variants that do not age. We discuss the consistency and explanatory power of this hypothesis and compare it with classic hypotheses of aging.

---

## Highlights

- The evolutionary origins and the mechanisms of aging are unclear.
- Most researchers believe aging is an entropic process or a detrimental side effect of some beneficial traits but not an adaptive program.
- A limited lifespan might provide benefits by preventing epidemics of infectious diseases.
- We propose that pathogen–host interactions are the primary reason for the evolution of adaptive programmed aging.
- Knowledge of the evolutionary origins of aging may instruct strategies to decipher its mechanisms and develop anti-aging interventions.

## Classic hypotheses of aging

Aging (see Glossary), defined broadly as an increase in mortality with age, is an evolutionary paradox [1]. Classic hypotheses consider mortality risks and physiological trade-offs as the main evolutionary determinants of aging (Figure 1A) [2–7]. In one of the earliest evolutionary hypotheses, the **selection shadow theory**, Medawar [2] and Haldane [3] considered senescence as a neutral or near-neutral trait existing because animals do not survive to old age due to extrinsic reasons of mortality (e.g., predation, cold, hunger). However, mortality increases with age in most species, suggesting that senescence is widespread in the wild and cannot be neutral [8,9].

Considering these observations, Williams introduced the **antagonistic pleiotropy theory** [4], inspired by Hamilton's seminal work [5]. It postulated pleiotropic genes that improve fitness in young years and reduce it in older ages. Younger groups dominate the demographic structures of most animal populations. Thus, early fitness is always under stronger selection [10], and hypothetical pleiotropic genes should be fixed by evolution.

Kirkwood further developed this concept by suggesting a general trade-off between young and old fitness. His **disposable soma theory** proposes that aging is an energy-saving mechanism that allows investment in reproduction at the expense of maintenance and longevity [6].

The latter two models are often grouped as 'pay later' hypotheses: young organisms 'borrow' fitness to pay it back in older ages. While being internally consistent, 'pay later' concepts require substantial initial assumptions. Antagonistic pleiotropy implies the existence of pleiotropic genes with age-dependent dual functions that evolution cannot disentangle. According to the expectations, genetic mutations that extend lifespan must lead to declines in early-life fitness, and indeed such observations have been made [11]. However, why can evolution not compensate for the detrimental effects without disturbing their beneficial functions, given the extreme genetic and genomic plasticity (Box 1)?

The disposable soma hypothesis seemingly resolves this problem by proposing energy as the currency of 'transferring' fitness from older to younger ages. It resonates nicely with the widespread **fecundity–longevity trade-off** found by comparing different species' life histories [12]. However, it also requires strong assumptions (Box 2) and contradicts some important observations [7,13]. Why do we not see non-aging animals in rich environments where nutrients are sufficient for maintenance and reproduction? Why do we see a conditional lifespan prolongation during caloric restriction [14] but not when food sources are excessive? Why do mammalian males age faster [15] while investing less in reproduction than females?

Several mechanistic **damage accumulation** models developed in parallel. These suggest that variable types of molecular damage are the etiological cause of aging. Damaging agents include reactive oxygen species, chemical modifications, somatic or germline mutations, selfish evolution of mitochondria, malignant cells, and the activity of transposons [16]. These models consider senescence an inevitable property of living matter, somehow intertwined into biochemistry and cell biology [17]. Damage accumulation concepts run into a contradiction with the evolutionary plasticity of longevity. For example, lifespan correlates with body and brain sizes [18], and since almost every taxonomic group contains large animals, longevity might have evolved many times independently. In primates and whales, longevity evolved rapidly, according to paleontology-based extrapolations of body and brain sizes [18]. Albeit a theoretical mechanistic limit for lifespan might exist, it cannot explain why short-lived animals have not evolved damage control mechanisms comparable with their long-lived relatives, given that selection should favor longevity in most of these species.

It seems that none of these evolutionary hypotheses can provide a general explanation for the evolution of lifespan set points, and a consensus on the evolutionary and mechanistic nature of aging is still missing [1,7].

## Host's lifespan in epidemiological models

Here we suggest that infectious diseases could shape the evolution of lifespan. Pathogens are one of the strongest genetic selection forces on Earth. Genes involved in the immune response evolve substantially faster than the rest of the genome [19,20]. Viruses alone were estimated to account for ~30% of all adaptive amino acid substitutions in the conserved part of the human proteome [21]. Thus, host–pathogen interactions might be responsible for a significant proportion of adaptive events. Could this robust and ubiquitous selective force explain phenomena not typically associated with infectious diseases, such as aging?

The host's lifespan is an essential parameter in epidemiological models [22]. If hosts recover from the disease and become resistant, the lifespan-dependent persistence of these immune individuals will affect epidemiological dynamics. If the disease is chronic and does not kill, the host's lifespan defines the duration of infection and the pathogen's transmission. If the host's longevity affects the evolution of pathogens, could epidemics reciprocally influence the evolution of lifespan? Short-lived individuals die earlier and transmit fewer chronic pathogens, thus benefiting their neighbors. In **viscous populations** with limited dispersal, neighbors might be related. Therefore, the death of an infected individual might be adaptive to its kin that would otherwise get an infection. Two initial attempts to demonstrate this benefit in epidemiological models found that a shorter lifespan is beneficial; however, the assumptions considered in these initial models somewhat limited their ability to provide a general theory for the evolution of aging [23–25].

Kirchner and Roy's model [23] was designed as a clustered host's **metapopulation** with random dispersal between the clusters and a constantly running severe epidemic of chronic host-sterilizing disease. Short-lived individuals transmit less. Hence, clusters populated by short-lived individuals were less infested by disease and the density of individuals in these clusters was higher. The authors suggested random disease-independent elimination of whole clusters. Due to its greater population numbers, the short-lived strain appeared to be more capable of repopulating these emptied niches and, over time, replaced the long-lived strain.

Mitteldorf and Pepper's model considered aging as a mechanism for population density control [24]. They studied the epidemics of a disease with 100% mortality and very high transmission rates in a **square lattice** model. If the host's density was high, the disease propagated quickly, killing a large proportion of the population. Shorter host lifespan or lower host fecundity reduced the density, leaving some sites vacant. These host-free regions prevented the epidemics, like areas cleared of trees prevent forest fires (percolation effects).

Both models assume some characteristics of the host population structure that render the models as special cases. In the first, a clustered metapopulation displayed sporadic disease-independent extinction of all individuals in whole clusters. In the second, host movement would destroy percolation effects [25]. Both models require permanent epidemics of severe pathogens that impose robust selection for resistance. Acquisition of resistance would make short lifespan redundant. Both models imply **group selection** [26], which is thought to occur only under limited conditions.

Overall, this body of literature does not support the idea that pathogens are a universal selective force for the evolution of aging.

## Pathogen control hypothesis

More recently, we reconsidered the idea that pathogen control could indeed be a main evolutionary determinant of lifespan set points [27]. Our approach was to assume conditions that are commonly observed during infection. We simulated host individuals' behavior in uniform viscous populations with limited dispersal and studied epidemics in this setup (Box 3). Our model generated a series of realistic conditions to explore how chronic infections could affect the evolution of long lifespan.

On emergence, a long-lived mutant individual initially propagates due to its extended reproductive period. Due to limited dispersal, an area with an abundance of long-lived variants forms. However, long-lived individuals are exposed to pathogens for a longer time, so their probability of harvesting pathogens is higher. In addition, chronic infections allow the host to survive for a long period of time, which contributes to the spread of the pathogen. Hence, the prevalence of chronic diseases is higher in regions inhabited predominantly by long-lived individuals. High pathogen load might cause local demographic depression if infections reduce host fecundity [28]. By contrast, short-lived individual populations should harvest fewer pathogens and therefore reproduce faster. This should result in demographic pressure for healthy short-lived individuals that reproduce faster and may enhance their dispersal into sparsely populated regions, resulting in the displacement of long-lived infected strains.

As suggested before [23], lifespan is defined as a trade-off between the benefits of an extended reproductive period if the host is healthy and the penalty of longer pathogen spread if it is infected. Unlike previous models, our framework is based on **kin selection** [26]. It does not involve multilevel selection, cooperation between non-relative animals, definition of groups, or other attributes of group-selection models. We identify territoriality [29] and **density-dependent dispersal** [30] (e.g., bacterial 'quorum sensing') as determinants of lifespan selection. Both behavioral patterns are common in nature.

Our hypothesis involves pathogens that reduce host reproductive capacity. Broadly, sterilization includes the inability to conceive and deliver, insufficient childcare, and debilitating vertical disease transmission. Generally, infections are expected to reduce host productivity since pathogens hijack resources that otherwise would be invested in reproduction [22]. Some pathogens use castration as a part of their reproductive strategy in invertebrates and fish, and can strongly reduce population density [28]. Infertility in humans can result from many chronic viral and bacterial diseases [31–33]. Our modeling shows that only partial sterilization might be sufficient to shape the evolution of lifespan [27].

Still, a constant epidemic of a severe disease might drive rapid evolution of the host's resistance as in previous models [23,24]. To relax this condition and to extend the relevant pathogen range, we proposed two additional scenarios (Figure 1B).

1. **Zoonotic pathogens** might impose selection pressure towards a shorter lifespan. Spillover infections might require time to adapt to a new species; therefore, long-lived variants should initiate more epidemics than short-lived ones [27]. Hosts interact with spillover pathogens for a short period and the problem with the evolution of resistance is relaxed.

2. **Co-infection** of several mildly chronic pathogens might produce cumulative sterilization that prevents the evolution of a longer lifespan, essentially as in the case of a single strongly sterilizing pathogen [27]. Since resistance alleles might differ for different infections, each mild disease produces only a limited selection for the host's resistance.

Thus, we propose a realistic and common combination of conditions that drive the evolution of limited lifespan as primitive and universal protection against chronic infectious diseases. This model does not involve stringent conditions proposed by previous models and suggests that pathogens could be a potential primary selective power shaping the evolution of lifespan.

## Critique of the concept of programmed adaptive aging

Regardless of the lack of agreement on the fundamental questions on the evolution of aging [1], it is nearly universally accepted that senescence cannot be an adaptive program by itself [4,13,25]. The pathogen control hypothesis addresses the specific criticisms raised against adaptive aging.

### Argument 1
If aging is adaptive, it is an altruistic suicide and should satisfy Hamilton's inequation: **br > c**, where **b** is a benefit for a recipient, **c** is the cost for an altruistic donor, and **r** is the relatedness between the two [34]. Since in most cases r < 1, c should be less than b, and because c, in the case of aging, is the cost of the individual's life, senescence should provide a huge unexplained benefit that exceeds this cost and the benefit should go to the related organisms specifically.

**Answer 1:** Confusion often comes from considering Hamilton's rule as a description of a pairwise interaction between donor and recipient organisms. In the case of epidemics, the inequation becomes **∑bnrn > c**, where the left part contains a sum of benefits for multiple individuals that an altruist might assist by preventing or curbing the epidemics. This sum might exceed the cost of an altruist's life.

### Argument 2
If senescence is adaptive, our genomes should encode genes executing the aging program. Why do we not see **biologically immortal** 'escape mutants' resulting from the inactivation of these genes [25]?

**Answer 2:** Long lifespan is advantageous only in the short term before disease propagation, but in the long term, after the spread of infection, it becomes a handicap because of the higher local prevalence of sterilizing pathogens. Under the realistic assumptions in our simulations, the parental group that produced this long-lived mutant is first replaced by this mutant, which is eliminated by pathogens as the epidemic progress (see Figure IG in Box 3).

Thus, the ability to evolve a long-lived mutant might be a detrimental trait itself, and selection might act against it. Altenberg [35] proposed that **evolvability** suppressors might prevent the emergence of short-term beneficial/long-term harmful alleles and stabilize far-sight adaptations. Altenberg's selection might explain the existence of mechanisms that prevent the emergence of long-lived escapers. The simplest mechanistic model for an evolvability suppressor would be a genetic linkage between a hypothetical lifespan controller and some vital functions. Such suppressors might reduce the fitness of long-lived mutants and prevent their expansion in populations. Importantly, this idea rationalizes the detrimental effects of lifespan-prolonging mutations that are often considered as evidence supporting the antagonistic pleiotropy theory [4,11]. The pathogen control hypothesis interprets these linkages as adaptive suppressors of evolvability selected to stabilize limited lifespan as a far-sight adaptation [27,35].

Thus, the pathogen control hypothesis allows aging to be a programmed adaptive mechanism of lifespan termination.

## The explanatory power of pathogen control hypothesis

The several hypotheses of aging differ in their abilities to explain aging (Table 1). Here we discuss several relevant examples.

### Fecundity–longevity trade-off
Pathogen control can explain the fecundity–longevity trade-off. The evolution of a shorter lifespan is possible only if the uninfected short-lived strain replicates faster than the long-lived strain sterilized by pathogens. Strong sterilization might reduce birth rates to small numbers independent of fertility in a healthy state. Thus, an increase in healthy-state fecundity would mostly favor short-lived individuals' reproduction, accelerating the elimination of the long-lived variant (see Equation I in Box 3) [27].

### Flying animals
Flying birds and bats live longer than flightless birds and terrestrial mammals [36]. The classical interpretation is that flyers efficiently avoid predators [4], thus decreasing **extrinsic mortality**. However, more careful research showed that levels of age-independent mortality should not directly affect the evolution of lifespan [37]. In some cases, higher mortality might select for longevity [38]. Finally, many bird species have high annual mortality [39], leaving the association between longevity and flight unexplained. The pathogen control hypothesis assumes that flying animals have reduced population viscosity because of extended dispersal distances [40,41]. If transmission of pathogens is less kin specific in flying animals, selection against longevity may be reduced [27].

### Eusocial animals
Another puzzling observation comes from analysis of the lifespans of **eusocial** animals. Breeders among bees, ants, termites, and some mole-rat species live substantially longer than workers, while having identical genomes [42,43]. We suggest that alleles increasing lifespan only in breeders would have a selective advantage because of the extended reproductive period. Pathogen control can be mediated by the shorter lifespan of workers that comprise the majority of susceptible hosts. Strikingly, parasitic tapeworms dramatically extend the lifespan of ant workers [44], supporting the notion that worker longevity might be beneficial for pathogens.

Moreover, eusociality might improve the evolutionary stability of workers' rapid adaptive suicide (i.e., **phenoptosis** [45]) in response to infection. This strategy might eliminate all of the pathogens from the populations, making aging redundant in all castes. We speculate that this might explain the extreme longevity of naked mole-rats (*Heterocephalus glaber*) [46] (Table 1).

## Concluding remarks

The pathogen control hypothesis of aging [27] offers several advantages. It is based on realistic assumptions and involves epidemics of chronic diseases as a ubiquitous and robust selective factor. By claiming aging as a programmed process, it addresses arguments historically raised against the adaptive aging hypotheses. Its explanatory power is stronger than that of the classic theories (Table 1) and, according to the standard epistemological criteria, should be considered a possible alternative. As a final advantage, the predictions produced by this hypothesis can be experimentally tested (see Outstanding questions).

## Outstanding questions

The pathogen control hypothesis produces several specific predictions that can be tested to support or refute the concept.

- Do sterilizing pathogens provide sufficient pressure to select for shorter lifespan? What is the role of pathogens with frequency-dependent transmission, in which transmission does not depend of population density (e.g., sexually transmitted pathogens)? Further, pathogen-induced sterilization is the critical element of the hypothesis, and studies of this aspect are essential to test it.

- Does infection between relatives occur more often than between random population members? Do structures of infection networks correlate with longevity? Do family and social structures affect infection networks and the evolution of lifespan? Can population structures explain longevity in highly mobile (e.g., flying) animals? Can population structures explain the asymmetry in longevity between different castes of eusocial species?

- Can protection from vertical pathogen transmission explain the evolution of **semelparity**?

- Are naked mole-rats hypersensitive to infection? The pathogen control hypothesis suggests that adaptive suicide (phenoptosis) in response to infection may be an alternative way to control pathogens that can make aging redundant. We expect naked mole-rats to be very susceptible to diseases and the workers to be less tolerant than the queens.

- Can the pathogen control hypothesis explain the difference in lifespans between genders, given that pathogens might differently sterilize the sexes? What are the effects of mating systems?

- How does senescence interact with the immune system? Immunity is dysregulated with age (immunosenescence). Aging can be accelerated by chronic infections or immune activation. What are the ecological outcomes of these interactions? Can they be adaptive?

- What is the taxonomic breadth of the pathogen control hypothesis? For example, can it address the evolution of lifespan in plants and fungi?

## Glossary

**Agent-based models:** models simulating the behavior and interactions of individual autonomous units (e.g., animals); widely used in epidemiology along with ordinary differential equation models.

**Aging:** here refers to an increase of all-cause mortality with age; in physiology, a complex of age-related maladaptive changes. Senescence is used as synonym.

**Antagonistic pleiotropy:** a major hypothesis proposing that aging results from late, detrimental activity of genes that were beneficial in youth. Such genes were named 'pleiotropic genes' (Box 1).

**Biologically immortal:** all-cause mortality does not depend on age.

**Damage accumulation:** a group of mechanistic hypotheses that explain aging as the incremental acquisition of molecular lesions.

**Demographic structure:** describes the age distribution of the population. A typical representation is a pyramid with youngest ages at the base and the oldest at the top. Fecundity and mortality define the shapes of these pyramids.

**Density-dependent dispersal:** movements of animals dictated by population pressures.

**Disposable soma:** postulates aging as the result of a trade-off between energy investments in reproduction and maintenance (Box 2).

**Eusociality:** social structure in which typically only the breeders reproduce and other castes are reversibly or irreversibly sterile. Found in some insects (ants, bees, termites), mammals (mole-rats), and crustaceans.

**Evolvability:** an organism's capacity to generate heritable phenotypic variation or produce a particular phenotypic variant.

**Extrinsic mortality or age-independent mortality:** the probability of dying due to hunger, predation, disease, or other environmental challenges.

**Fecundity–longevity trade-off:** negative correlation between number of progeny and lifespan, empirically observed when comparing different animal species.

**Fitness:** the number of offspring that survive to maturity.

**Group selection:** a type of selection that presumes evolution to act on the level of the group. Requires stable cooperative interactions between non-relative individuals. It is considered to be a rare phenomenon.

**Kin selection:** a widespread type of selection driving the altruistic behavior towards related individuals. Parental care is an example of kin selection.

**Metapopulation:** a population of populations. Interactions between individuals within the cluster are more frequent (or are somewhat different) than interactions with individuals from other clusters.

**Mortality:** the likelihood of dying per unit of time.

**Phenoptosis:** adaptive death of an organism in response to a stimulus [45] (e.g., apoptosis in unicellular organisms).

**Pleiotropic genes:** in the context of aging, genes that improve fitness in young years and reduce it at older ages.

**Quorum sensing:** regulation of gene expression in response to fluctuations in cell-population density in bacteria. In some cases, it leads to migration from densely populated areas.

**Selection shadow:** this concept explains a decline of selection power for the traits expressed at later ages. If only a few animals survive to a given age, selection might be less stringent.

**Semelparity:** replication regime with a single reproduction event, followed by the parent's death.

**Square lattice:** chessboard-like lattice in which agents are placed in the nodes and typically interact with their four or eight neighbors. It is used for modeling spatial processes, including epidemics.

**Viscous population:** individuals are not interacting promiscuously due to limited dispersal; interactions between relatives occur more often.

## Box 1. Can pleiotropic genes explain aging?

Williams postulated genes with opposite effects on fitness at different ages [4] contemporary with the nucleation of molecular genetics. However, this concept cannot account for the genomic variability and plasticity that became apparent decades later. To our knowledge, there is no convincing explanation for why detrimental effects of pleiotropic genes cannot be removed or compensated by mutations in other regions of the genome. Williams' example proposed that rapid bone calcification is required for faster organism growth and might be associated with arteriosclerosis in later years [4]. While still cited in the field [13,47], this trade-off is not universal: long-lived bird species grow faster than most short-lived mammals [48].

Another example considers cellular senescence as an anticancer mechanism and systemic aging as a detrimental side effect of neoplasia prevention [49]. However, large animals control their malignancies in more cells for a longer time, suggesting they evolved more efficient cancer surveillance combined with extended longevity (Peto's paradox) [50]. Thus, this trade-off is not fundamental and can be uncoupled.

Next, resistance to pathogens was suggested as a source of pleiotropic genes [51,52]. Detrimental alleles responsible for sickle-cell anemia or cystic fibrosis might be represented in human populations since they provide partial resistance to malaria and tuberculosis, respectively [53,54]. Could aging result from a detrimental side effect of some pathogen-resistance genes? While the hypothesis is appealing, the experimental evidence suggests mutations that extend lifespan often increase resistance to pathogens, indicating that a negative trade-off between longevity and general immunity does not exist [55–57]. Might pleiotropic genes be fixed by selection coming from only a small subset of very severe diseases, such as malaria? In this situation, we would expect the manifestation of aging to be variable between species or even populations of the same species exposed to various diseases (e.g., symptoms of sickle-cell anemia and cystic fibrosis differ dramatically). However, senescence is surprisingly conserved in most species, making this model unlikely [58].

In most other instances, hypothetical pleiotropic effects bring us to the disposable soma hypothesis (Box 2), since the penalty can be compensated by additional energy investment. For example, if a beneficial activity of oxidative phosphorylation leads to detrimental reactive oxygen species production, the latter effect can be ameliorated by energy investment into elevated catalase expression.

Thus, no unambiguous examples of pleiotropic genes have been proposed so far, undermining the critical assumption of Williams' hypothesis.

## Box 2. Can aging be an energy-saving strategy?

Disposable soma theory proposes that aging results from attempts to optimize energy investments between maintenance and fecundity [6]. Short-lived organisms that invest in early reproduction outgrow long-lived slow replicators. An energetic value already invested in the parental organism's body will not contribute to its alleles' fitness when it dies, but higher fecundity in the early years might compensate for this. Therefore, disposable soma theory proposes that aging increases replication speed while staying on the fixed energy budget rather than optimizing energy spending.

However, selection does not always favor replication speed. Let us assume that a population of animals reaches its carrying capacity and that food is a limiting factor. In that case, there might be no benefit in increasing the birth rate: juveniles are typically more vulnerable to starvation than adults and the extra offspring might not survive. To keep the representation of its genes in the genetic pool, an individual must have at least one offspring that reaches adulthood. Under these conditions, a parent's death by aging might save energy only if growing a new organism from a zygote to sexual maturity is cheaper than maintaining the parent's body. This condition contradicts the law of energy conservation: creating a new individual should always be more expensive than replacing some damaged cells or proteins of the pre-existing parental organism. Thus, disposable soma cannot explain aging in stagnant populations where replication is less important than survival.

In constantly growing populations, disposable soma might explain aging by preferential investment in reproduction speed. Regimes of prolonged population growth are not ubiquitous but might exist. The critical requirement for such a scenario is an excess of nutrients to sustain the exponentially increasing cohort of animals. This condition contradicts one of the premises of the disposable soma theory, which posits that the energy budget is fixed since food is restricted. If individuals of different ages feed on more or less the same food source, disposable soma runs into an internal contradiction: the environment should be sufficient to sustain growing progeny and simultaneously restricted when it comes to the adult's maintenance. Therefore, disposable soma might work only if: (i) the population grows and (ii) juveniles have privileged food access compared with adults. We believe that this scenario cannot be ubiquitous and disposable soma cannot explain aging as a universal phenomenon.

## Box 3. Modeling evolution of lifespan shaped by epidemics

We modeled [27] the epidemics of chronic sterilizing diseases in a population of individuals who die rapidly after reaching the age of senescence.

We considered territorial animals that compete for areas to maximize access to spatially distributed resources (e.g., food, water, shelter). **Agents** moved across 2D space and pushed each other away, forming a flexible lattice that 'breathes' due to the deaths and births. An individual's fecundity depended on the available resources and was proportional to the controlled area. Therefore, individuals in sparsely populated areas were more productive than those in dense regions. Interactions between relatives occurred more frequently than between random individuals. Dispersal was gradual and density-dependent [30]: individuals from densely populated areas migrated into the less-inhabited regions.

We simulated the emergence of long-lived mutants in the population of hosts with a shorter lifespan. In the presence of chronic host-sterilizing disease with slow transmission, the short-lived strain outcompeted the mutant. The model described the following scenario of selection:

1. A long-lived strain propagates until it encounters the pathogen.
2. Infected long-lived individuals survive and spread the disease for longer periods, resulting in higher pathogen prevalence in their local niches and in
3. Sterilization and a demographic depression.
4. Short-lived individuals from uninfected, highly productive regions disperse into these sparse areas and completely replace the long-lived mutant variant.

Local demographic depression induced by long-lived variant propagation might be harmful for neighboring short-lived individuals. The mutant displaces its adjacent short-lived counterparts during initial expansion and is then wiped out by infection. This effect posits extended lifespan as a short-term beneficial, far-term detrimental trait that promotes the evolution of evolvability suppressors [35] (see main text).

A necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the selection of a shorter lifespan is that the population growth rate of the short-lived (SL) variant exceeds that of the long-lived (LL). The growth rate is proportional to:

**B·(1−P·n) − 1/A**

where **B** is the fecundity, **P** is the degree of sterilization by the pathogen, **n** is the proportion of infected individuals, and **A** is the lifespan. The shorter lifespan evolves if the growth rate inequality holds, suggesting that it is more efficient at higher B, P, and (nLL − nSL), which reflects the asymmetry of pathogen prevalence, and at low Δ (the ratio ALL/ASL).
